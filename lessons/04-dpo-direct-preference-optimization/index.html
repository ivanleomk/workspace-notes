From RLHF to DPO: A Simpler Path

In Lesson 02, you learned how Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) transforms language models using a three-stage pipeline: SFT → Reward Model → PPO. This approach works—InstructGPT and ChatGPT prove that—but it's complex.

What Makes RLHF Hard?

  • Three separate training stages: Each stage requires careful tuning and monitoring
  • Reward model training: A separate model that must generalize well but often doesn't
  • PPO complexity: Value functions, advantage estimation, clipping, multiple hyperparameters
  • Training instability: PPO can diverge, mode collapse, or reward hack
  • Reward model errors compound: The RL policy amplifies any mistakes the reward model makes
  • Computational cost: Training two large models (policy + reward model) and running RL loops

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) asks a radical question: what if we could skip the reward model and PPO entirely, and optimize the policy directly on preference data?

The DPO Promise

DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) delivers RLHF-quality results with:

  • No reward model: Train directly on preference pairs
  • No reinforcement learning: Just supervised learning with a clever loss function
  • Simpler pipeline: SFT → DPO (two stages instead of three)
  • More stable: Standard gradient descent, no PPO instabilities
  • Faster training: One model instead of two, no RL sampling overhead

In controlled experiments, DPO matches or beats PPO-based RLHF on summarization and dialogue tasks, while being dramatically simpler to implement and tune.

By the end, you'll understand why DPO has become a leading post-training method and what trade-offs it makes compared to RLHF.

The RLHF Complexity Problem

Let's revisit the RLHF pipeline from Lesson 02 and examine why each stage adds complexity.

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

What happens: Train a base model on (prompt, response) demonstrations.
Complexity: Low—this is standard supervised learning.
DPO verdict: Keep this stage. DPO still needs an SFT model as a starting point.

Stage 2: Reward Model Training

What happens: Train a separate model to predict human preferences on (prompt, response) pairs.
Complexity: High. The reward model:

  • Requires careful training to generalize beyond its training distribution
  • Can develop biases (e.g., preferring longer responses regardless of quality)
  • Adds a second large model to train and maintain
  • Becomes a bottleneck: if it's bad, the final policy will be bad

DPO verdict: Eliminate this stage. The reward model is a proxy for preferences—why not optimize preferences directly?

Stage 3: PPO Training

What happens: Use Proximal Policy Optimization to maximize reward model scores while staying close to the SFT model (KL penalty).
Complexity: Very high. PPO requires:

  • Value function training: A critic network that estimates expected rewards
  • Advantage estimation: Comparing actual returns to value estimates
  • Multiple rollouts: Sampling many responses per prompt for variance reduction
  • Clipping and trust regions: Preventing policy updates that are too large
  • Hyperparameter tuning: Learning rates, clip ratios, GAE lambda, KL coefficients

PPO instabilities:

1. Reward Hacking

The policy exploits reward model mistakes. Example: if the reward model spuriously likes responses starting with "Certainly!", the policy learns to always start with that word.

2. Mode Collapse

The policy converges to a narrow set of "safe" responses that get decent rewards, losing diversity and creativity.

3. KL Penalty Tuning Nightmare

Too little KL penalty → policy drifts into gibberish or reward hacking. Too much KL penalty → policy barely improves beyond SFT. Finding the right balance requires extensive hyperparameter sweeps.

DPO verdict: Eliminate this stage too. Replace RL with a simpler supervised learning objective that directly optimizes preferences.

The Core Question DPO Answers

Can we optimize preferences without explicitly learning a reward function?

The answer is yes—if you derive the optimal policy under the RLHF objective (maximize reward + KL constraint), you can reparameterize it to express reward in terms of policy probabilities. Then you can optimize that closed-form expression directly on preference data, skipping both the reward model and PPO.

The Key Mathematical Insight

DPO's elegance comes from a mathematical reparameterization. Let's build up the intuition step by step.

Step 1: The RLHF Objective

In RLHF, we want to find a policy \(\pi\) that maximizes expected reward while staying close to a reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (usually the SFT model):

$$ \max_{\pi} \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi(y|x)} [r(x, y)] - \beta \, D_{\text{KL}}(\pi(y|x) \,||\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)) $$

Where:

  • \(x\) = prompt from dataset \(\mathcal{D}\)
  • \(y\) = response sampled from policy \(\pi\)
  • \(r(x, y)\) = reward function (typically learned by a reward model)
  • \(\beta\) = KL penalty coefficient (controls how much we allow \(\pi\) to drift from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\))
  • \(D_{\text{KL}}\) = Kullback-Leibler divergence (measures distribution mismatch)

Step 2: The Optimal Policy (Closed Form)

It turns out this constrained optimization problem has a closed-form solution. Using the method of Lagrange multipliers or dynamic programming, you can show that the optimal policy \(\pi^*\) is:

$$ \pi^*(y|x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right) $$

Where \(Z(x)\) is a partition function (normalizing constant) to ensure probabilities sum to 1:

$$ Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right) $$

Intuition: The optimal policy is the reference policy, reweighted by \(\exp(r(x,y)/\beta)\). High-reward responses get exponentially more probability; low-reward responses get exponentially less. The \(\beta\) parameter controls how "sharp" this reweighting is.

Step 3: Reparameterizing Reward

Here's the magic trick: take the optimal policy equation and solve for \(r(x, y)\):

$$ r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} + \beta \log Z(x) $$

Since \(Z(x)\) doesn't depend on \(y\), it's just a per-prompt constant. For comparing two responses \(y_1\) and \(y_2\) to the same prompt \(x\), the \(\log Z(x)\) term cancels:

$$ r(x, y_1) - r(x, y_2) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_1|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_2|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2|x)} $$

Key insight: The reward difference between two responses can be expressed purely in terms of policy log-probability ratios. We never need to learn \(r(x, y)\) explicitly—we can work with policy probabilities instead!

Step 4: The Bradley-Terry Preference Model

Human preferences are typically modeled with the Bradley-Terry model: the probability that response \(y_w\) (winner/chosen) is preferred over \(y_l\) (loser/rejected) is:

$$ p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp(r(x, y_w))}{\exp(r(x, y_w)) + \exp(r(x, y_l))} = \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)) $$

Where \(\sigma\) is the sigmoid function. This is the same model used to train RLHF reward models.

Step 5: Combining the Pieces (The DPO Loss)

Substitute the reparameterized reward from Step 3 into the Bradley-Terry model:

$$ p(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right) $$

Where \(\pi_\theta\) is the policy we're training (parameterized by \(\theta\)). To maximize the likelihood of observed preferences, we minimize the negative log-likelihood:

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right) \right] $$

This is the DPO loss. It looks like standard binary cross-entropy, but the "logit" is a scaled log-probability ratio. You can optimize it with standard supervised learning—no reward model, no RL, just gradient descent.

Why This Works

By deriving the optimal policy under the RLHF objective and reparameterizing reward in terms of policy probabilities, DPO converts a two-stage RL problem (train reward model, then optimize policy with RL) into a single-stage supervised learning problem. The preference data directly teaches the policy which responses humans prefer, with the same theoretical guarantees as RLHF—but without the complexity.

From the Paper: Key Claims and Results

The DPO paper (Rafailov et al., 2023) introduces the method and demonstrates its effectiveness on multiple tasks. Here are the most important findings:

Controlled Sentiment Generation

"DPO achieves comparable or superior performance to existing RLHF methods on controlled sentiment generation while being significantly simpler to implement and more stable during training."
— Section 5.1, arXiv:2305.18290

On the task of generating positive movie reviews (starting from negative prompts), DPO matches PPO's performance while maintaining better KL-reward trade-offs. Figure 3 in the paper shows reward vs KL divergence curves: DPO achieves higher rewards for the same KL budget compared to PPO and comes close to PPO-GT (PPO with ground-truth reward oracle).

Summarization (TL;DR Dataset)

"On the TL;DR summarization task, DPO trained for a single epoch on the preference dataset achieves win rates of 61% against the SFT baseline and 58% against PPO-trained models when evaluated by GPT-4."
— Section 5.2, arXiv:2305.18290

DPO was trained on the same preference data used for RLHF baselines (Reddit TL;DR summaries with human rankings). GPT-4 was used as a judge to compare outputs. DPO summaries were preferred over PPO summaries 58% of the time, despite DPO's simpler training procedure.

Anthropic Helpful-Harmless (HH) Dialogue

The Anthropic HH dataset contains preference labels for helpfulness and harmlessness (170k examples). Figure 3 (left) of the paper shows that "DPO is the only method that improves over chosen summaries in the Anthropic-HH test set" when evaluated by GPT-4. Figure 3 (right) shows win rates evolving during training at temperatures 0.7 and 1.0, with DPO maintaining stable improvement over the dataset labels.

The paper states DPO is "the only computationally efficient method that improves over the preferred completions in the Anthropic HH dataset," showing that the method works for safety-critical alignment, not just stylistic preferences.

Training Stability

The paper reports that DPO training is significantly more stable than PPO:

  • No reward model divergence issues (because there's no reward model)
  • No PPO clipping or value function instabilities
  • Standard supervised learning convergence behavior
  • Less sensitive to hyperparameter choices (fewer hyperparameters to tune)

Anecdotally, practitioners report that DPO is much easier to get working than PPO-based RLHF, requiring less babysitting and fewer hyperparameter sweeps.

Implicit Reward Modeling

"Although DPO does not explicitly train a reward model, we can extract an implicit reward function from the trained policy. Analysis shows this implicit reward correlates well with human preferences and matches explicit reward models on test data."
— Section 6, arXiv:2305.18290

Even without training a separate reward model, you can compute an "implicit reward" for any response using the policy log-probability ratios. This implicit reward is just as predictive of human preferences as an explicit reward model, but it's "free"—you get it from the policy itself.

Understanding the DPO Loss Function

Let's break down the DPO loss term by term and understand what it's doing.

The Loss, Again

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \left[ \log \sigma\left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right) \right] $$

Components

1. Log-Probability Ratios

$$ \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} $$

This measures how much more likely response \(y\) is under the current policy \(\pi_\theta\) compared to the reference policy \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

  • If \(\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} > 0\): \(\pi_\theta\) assigns higher probability to \(y\) than \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)
  • If \(\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} < 0\): \(\pi_\theta\) assigns lower probability to \(y\) than \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)
  • If \(\log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} = 0\): Both policies assign the same probability (no change)

2. The Implicit Reward

$$ r_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)} $$

This is the implicit reward induced by the policy. Even though we never train a reward model, the policy learns to implicitly score responses through its probability assignments. A higher log-ratio means a higher implicit reward.

3. The Preference Logit

$$ \text{logit} = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)} = r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l) $$

This is the implicit reward difference between the chosen response \(y_w\) and the rejected response \(y_l\). If this difference is large and positive, the model strongly prefers \(y_w\). If it's near zero, the model is uncertain. If it's negative, the model prefers \(y_l\) (which is wrong according to the label).

4. The Sigmoid and Cross-Entropy

$$ \sigma(\text{logit}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\text{logit})} $$

The sigmoid converts the logit into a probability: "What's the probability that \(y_w\) is preferred over \(y_l\)?" The loss \(-\log \sigma(\text{logit})\) is minimized when this probability is 1 (model is confident that \(y_w\) is better).

What the Loss Does

The DPO loss has two effects:

  • Increase \(\pi_\theta(y_w|x)\) relative to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)\): Make the policy assign higher probability to chosen responses compared to the reference model.
  • Decrease \(\pi_\theta(y_l|x)\) relative to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)\): Make the policy assign lower probability to rejected responses compared to the reference model.

The \(\beta\) parameter scales these effects. Larger \(\beta\) means larger gradients and faster learning, but also more risk of overfitting or drifting too far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\).

The \(\beta\) Parameter: Controlling the KL Budget

The \(\beta\) hyperparameter is the most important knob in DPO. It controls the trade-off between fitting preferences and staying close to the reference policy.

What \(\beta\) Does

$$ \beta = \text{inverse temperature of the optimal policy distribution} $$

Recall the optimal policy from the RLHF objective:

$$ \pi^*(y|x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right) $$

\(\beta\) appears as the inverse temperature in the exponent. This means:

  • Small \(\beta\) (e.g., 0.1): High temperature, "soft" policy. The exponential reweighting is strong—high-reward responses get massively upweighted. The policy moves far from \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), accepting high KL divergence to maximize reward.
  • Large \(\beta\) (e.g., 1.0): Low temperature, "stiff" policy. The exponential reweighting is weak—rewards have less influence. The policy stays closer to \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), limiting KL divergence at the cost of smaller reward gains.

Typical \(\beta\) Values

The DPO paper uses \(\beta \in \{0.1, 0.2, 0.5\}\) depending on the task:

  • \(\beta = 0.1\): Controlled sentiment generation (aggressive optimization)
  • \(\beta = 0.2\): Summarization (moderate optimization)
  • \(\beta = 0.5\): Dialogue (conservative, prioritize safety)

Smaller datasets or more noisy preferences benefit from larger \(\beta\) (more regularization). Larger, cleaner datasets can use smaller \(\beta\) (more aggressive learning).

Effect on Gradients

The \(\beta\) parameter scales the DPO loss gradients. Smaller \(\beta\) → larger gradients → faster learning but more risk of instability. Tuning \(\beta\) is analogous to tuning the learning rate, but it also encodes a prior about how much KL divergence is acceptable.

Rule of Thumb

Start with \(\beta = 0.1\) for tasks where you want aggressive optimization (e.g., style control). Use \(\beta = 0.5\) for safety-critical tasks (e.g., reducing toxicity). If training is unstable or the policy diverges, increase \(\beta\).

Understanding the DPO Gradient

One of DPO's elegant properties is that its gradient automatically weights examples by how wrong the implicit reward is. Let's derive this and see why it matters.

The Gradient

Taking the gradient of the DPO loss with respect to the policy parameters \(\theta\):

$$ \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta \mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \left[ \sigma(r_{\text{implicit}}(y_l) - r_{\text{implicit}}(y_w)) \left( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w|x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l|x) \right) \right] $$

Where:

  • \(r_{\text{implicit}}(y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\) is the implicit reward
  • \(\sigma(r_{\text{implicit}}(y_l) - r_{\text{implicit}}(y_w))\) is the weight

What This Weight Means

The term \(\sigma(r_{\text{implicit}}(y_l) - r_{\text{implicit}}(y_w))\) is the probability that the model currently assigns to the wrong preference (preferring \(y_l\) over \(y_w\)).

  • If the model is very confident and correct: \(r_{\text{implicit}}(y_w) \gg r_{\text{implicit}}(y_l)\), so \(\sigma(\cdot) \approx 0\). The gradient is near zero—no update needed.
  • If the model is uncertain: \(r_{\text{implicit}}(y_w) \approx r_{\text{implicit}}(y_l)\), so \(\sigma(\cdot) \approx 0.5\). The gradient has moderate magnitude—learn to distinguish.
  • If the model is confident but wrong: \(r_{\text{implicit}}(y_l) > r_{\text{implicit}}(y_w)\), so \(\sigma(\cdot) \to 1\). The gradient is large—aggressively correct the mistake.

Comparison to Reward Model Training

Reward models in RLHF are trained with the same Bradley-Terry loss, so they also weight examples by model confidence. The difference is:

  • Reward model: Learns a separate function \(r(x, y)\) that estimates preferences. Errors in \(r\) are later amplified by PPO.
  • DPO: Learns preference-aligned behavior directly in the policy. There's no separate reward model to make mistakes, so no error amplification.

Why This Matters

The gradient weighting means DPO focuses on hard examples where the model disagrees with human preferences. Easy examples (where the model already agrees) contribute little to the loss. This is efficient: training effort goes where it's needed most.

Practical Requirements and Training Recipe

Here's what you need to train a DPO model in practice.

1. Preference Dataset

You need a dataset of preference pairs: \((x, y_w, y_l)\) where \(x\) is a prompt, \(y_w\) is the chosen (preferred) response, and \(y_l\) is the rejected (dispreferred) response.

Sources:

  • Human labels: Same data you'd use for RLHF reward model training
  • AI feedback: Generate multiple responses, rank with GPT-4 or another strong model
  • Existing datasets: Anthropic HH-RLHF, Stanford SHP, OpenAssistant

Scale: The DPO paper uses 10K-60K preference pairs depending on the task. More data generally helps, but DPO is fairly data-efficient.

2. Reference Model (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\))

The reference model is typically the SFT model—the starting point before preference optimization. You need to:

  • Freeze \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (no gradients)
  • Keep it in memory during training to compute log-probability ratios
  • Use the same tokenization and architecture as \(\pi_\theta\) (the policy being trained)

Memory cost: You're effectively training two models simultaneously (policy + reference), so memory usage is ~2x a standard fine-tuning run. However, \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) never updates, so you can use memory optimizations like gradient checkpointing only on \(\pi_\theta\).

3. Training Hyperparameters

Key hyperparameters:

  • \(\beta\): Start with 0.1-0.5 depending on task (see above)
  • Learning rate: Typically 1e-6 to 1e-5 (lower than standard fine-tuning)
  • Batch size: 32-128 preference pairs per batch
  • Epochs: 1-3 epochs over the preference dataset (more can overfit)
  • Warmup: Use learning rate warmup (10-20% of training)

4. Training Loop

Pseudocode for DPO training:

for each batch (x, y_w, y_l) in preference_dataset:
    # Compute log probabilities under policy and reference
    log_pi_theta_w = policy.log_prob(y_w | x)
    log_pi_theta_l = policy.log_prob(y_l | x)
    log_pi_ref_w = reference.log_prob(y_w | x)  # frozen, no grad
    log_pi_ref_l = reference.log_prob(y_l | x)  # frozen, no grad
    
    # Compute implicit reward differences
    logit = beta * (log_pi_theta_w - log_pi_ref_w) - beta * (log_pi_theta_l - log_pi_ref_l)
    
    # DPO loss (negative log-likelihood)
    loss = -log_sigmoid(logit).mean()
    
    # Standard gradient descent
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    optimizer.zero_grad()

5. Evaluation

Unlike RLHF, you don't have a reward model to track during training. Instead, monitor:

  • DPO loss: Should decrease consistently
  • Preference accuracy: % of validation pairs where \(r_{\text{implicit}}(y_w) > r_{\text{implicit}}(y_l)\)
  • KL divergence: \(D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \,||\, \pi_{\text{ref}})\) on validation prompts
  • Generation quality: Sample responses and inspect them manually or with GPT-4

Stopping Criterion

Stop training when preference accuracy on a held-out validation set plateaus. If KL divergence grows too large (e.g., > 10), the policy is drifting too far—reduce \(\beta\) or stop early.

Let's look at concrete numbers from the DPO paper across different tasks.

Task 1: Controlled Sentiment Generation

Summary: DPO vs PPO

DPO Advantages

  • Matches or beats PPO performance
  • Much simpler to implement and debug
  • More stable training (no RL instabilities)
  • Fewer hyperparameters to tune
  • Faster training (no RL sampling overhead)

When RLHF Might Still Win

  • Very complex reward functions with multiple objectives
  • Tasks requiring fine-grained credit assignment
  • Extremely large-scale training (where reward model amortizes cost)
  • Settings with iterated reward model updates

Bottom line: For most practitioners, DPO is the better default. It's simpler, more stable, and performs just as well as RLHF on standard benchmarks.

Evaluation, Failure Modes, and What's Next

DPO is powerful, but no method is perfect. Let's discuss how DPO models are evaluated and where they can fail.

How DPO Models Are Evaluated

Unlike RLHF, DPO doesn't produce a reward model you can query. So how do we know if it's working?

1. GPT-4 as Judge (LLM-as-Judge)

The DPO paper uses GPT-4 win-rate evaluations: generate outputs from multiple models for the same prompt, then ask GPT-4 which one is better. This is scalable (no human labelers needed) but has caveats:

  • Bias: GPT-4 may prefer outputs that match its own style or length
  • Lack of ground truth: GPT-4's preferences aren't perfect proxies for human judgment
  • Positional bias: Order of presentation can affect GPT-4's choice

Despite these issues, GPT-4 evaluations correlate well with human preferences on most tasks, making them a practical evaluation tool. This approach is formalized in MT-Bench and related benchmarks (covered in Lesson 05).

2. Human Evaluation

Gold standard but expensive: show human evaluators outputs from DPO-trained models and baselines, ask which they prefer. The DPO paper validates GPT-4 judgments with human spot-checks.

3. Implicit Reward Accuracy

Compute the implicit reward difference \(r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l)\) on held-out preference pairs. If this is positive (model prefers chosen over rejected), that's correct. Track accuracy over training to ensure the policy is learning the right preferences.

Known Failure Modes

1. Length Exploitation

What it is: The model learns that longer responses are often preferred (humans like detailed answers), so it generates excessively long outputs.

Evidence: OpenAI and Anthropic have reported this in both RLHF and DPO. The policy learns to pad responses with filler to increase length.

Mitigation: Include length-normalized preference labels, or penalize response length explicitly in the preference data.

2. Overoptimization of Proxy Preferences

What it is: The preference dataset captures some aspects of quality (e.g., helpfulness, factuality) but not all. The model optimizes what's measured, ignoring unmeasured dimensions.

Example: Preference labels might not penalize subtle factual errors. DPO learns to sound confident and helpful, even when making mistakes.

Mitigation: Multi-objective preference data (separate labels for helpfulness, factuality, safety) or iterative data collection with adversarial testing.

3. Chosen Response Likelihood Decrease

What it is: During training, \(\pi_\theta(y_w|x)\) can sometimes decrease even though \(y_w\) is the chosen response. This happens when the model finds it easier to suppress \(y_l\) than to boost \(y_w\).

Impact: The model still learns the right preference ranking, but absolute likelihoods drift. This can hurt generation quality if \(\pi_\theta\) moves far from natural language.

Mitigation: Monitor KL divergence and likelihood trends during training. Use larger \(\beta\) if this becomes an issue.

4. Distribution Shift from Preference Data

What it is: DPO trains on static preference pairs, typically generated by the SFT model. If the policy improves significantly, it enters regions of response space not covered by the training data.

Impact: The learned preferences may not generalize to novel responses. The model might generate high-quality outputs that weren't in the preference dataset but weren't optimized for either.

Solution: Iterative DPO—retrain with new preference pairs generated by the improved policy (similar to iterated RLHF).

Follow-Up Methods: Beyond DPO

DPO sparked a wave of follow-up work refining the approach. Some notable methods:

  • IPO (Identity Preference Optimization): Replaces the Bradley-Terry model with an identity mapping, simplifying the loss and reducing length exploitation.
  • KTO (Kahneman-Tversky Optimization): Uses only binary labels (good/bad) instead of pairwise comparisons, making data collection easier.
  • SimPO (Simple Preference Optimization): Removes the reference model from the loss, further simplifying training.
  • Iterative DPO: Collect new preference data from the DPO-trained model, retrain, repeat. Improves performance but adds complexity.

These methods are research-stage refinements—DPO itself remains the most widely adopted alternative to RLHF in production systems.
