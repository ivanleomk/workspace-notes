Beyond Multiple Choice: Judging Open-Ended Chat Quality

In Lessons 01 and 03, you learned how benchmarks like MMLU and GSM8K evaluate models using multiple-choice questions or verifiable answers. These formats have clear advantages: automatic scoring, objective correctness, large-scale testing. But they have a critical limitation—they don't capture what matters most for chat assistants.

What Multiple Choice Can't Measure

  • Helpfulness: Is the response actually useful to the user?
  • Instruction-following: Does the model do what the user asked?
  • Conversational quality: Is the response natural, clear, and appropriately detailed?
  • Multi-turn coherence: Can the model maintain context across a conversation?
  • Safety and harmlessness: Does the model avoid harmful or toxic outputs?

These dimensions require subjective human judgment, not just right/wrong answers. But human evaluation is expensive, slow, and doesn't scale to rapid model iteration.

LLM-as-a-Judge asks a provocative question: what if we use a powerful language model (like GPT-4) to evaluate other models' outputs? Can automated LLM judges approximate human preferences at scale?

The MT-Bench & Chatbot Arena Framework

Zheng et al. (2023) introduced two complementary evaluation systems:

  • MT-Bench: A curated benchmark of 80 multi-turn conversation prompts across 8 categories (writing, roleplay, reasoning, math, coding, STEM, humanities, extraction). GPT-4 judges model responses on a 1-10 scale.
  • Chatbot Arena: A crowdsourced platform where users chat with two anonymous models side-by-side and vote for which is better. Elo ratings emerge from thousands of pairwise comparisons.

Together, these systems enabled the first rigorous study of LLM judges: their agreement with humans, their biases (position, verbosity, self-preference), and how to mitigate those biases.

What You'll Learn

This lesson covers:

  • Why open-ended chat quality is hard to benchmark with multiple-choice tests
  • MT-Bench design: 80 multi-turn questions across 8 categories, two-turn structure
  • Chatbot Arena: crowdsourced pairwise votes, Elo ratings, Bradley-Terry model
  • Three LLM judging modes: pairwise comparison, single-answer grading, reference-guided grading
  • Judge biases: position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement bias
  • Mitigation strategies: swap positions, few-shot prompting, chain-of-thought, reference answers for math
  • Agreement results: GPT-4 vs human agreement (~80-85%), comparable to human-human agreement
  • Connection to post-training: how LLM judges and pairwise preferences enable RLHF/DPO, and pitfalls like reward hacking

By the end, you'll understand when LLM judges are reliable, what their failure modes are, and how they connect to the preference-based training methods from Lessons 02 and 04.

The Evaluation Gap: Why Multiple Choice Isn't Enough

Let's revisit benchmarks from earlier lessons and examine why they fall short for evaluating chat models.

MMLU (Lesson 01): Knowledge But Not Application

What it measures: Factual knowledge across 57 academic subjects via multiple choice.
What it misses: Can the model use that knowledge to help a user? A model might ace MMLU but produce verbose, unhelpful, or condescending responses when asked for practical advice.

GSM8K (Lesson 03): Reasoning But Not Explanation

What it measures: Multi-step reasoning on grade-school math problems with verifiable numeric answers.
What it misses: GSM8K doesn't measure whether the model explains its reasoning clearly, whether it catches its own errors, or whether it follows instructions (e.g., "explain this to a 10-year-old").

The Open-Ended Conversation Challenge

Real users don't ask multiple-choice questions. They ask:

  • "Help me write a cover letter for a software engineering job."
  • "What should I cook for dinner tonight? I have chicken, rice, and broccoli."
  • "Explain quantum entanglement like I'm five."
  • "Write a Python script to scrape a website." (Then: "Now add error handling.")

These prompts have no single correct answer. Quality is subjective: one user wants conciseness, another wants detail. Evaluating these requires human judgment—or a scalable proxy for it.

Why Not Just Use Humans?

Human evaluation is the gold standard, but it has serious limitations:

  • Expensive: $10-50+ per comparison depending on evaluator expertise
  • Slow: Days or weeks to collect hundreds of judgments
  • Doesn't scale: Training runs produce dozens of checkpoints; evaluating each is infeasible
  • Inconsistent: Human annotators disagree (~70-80% agreement on preference tasks)

LLM judges offer a potential solution: scalable, fast, and cheap evaluation that approximates human preferences. But do they work? That's what MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena measure.

MT-Bench: Multi-Turn Benchmark Design

MT-Bench (Multi-Turn Benchmark) is a curated set of 80 conversation prompts designed to test chat models on realistic, open-ended tasks.

Design Principles

1. Multi-Turn Conversations (Two Turns)

Each MT-Bench question has two turns:

  • Turn 1: Initial user request (e.g., "Write a short poem about a sunset")
  • Turn 2: Follow-up that builds on Turn 1 (e.g., "Now rewrite it in the style of Shakespeare")

This tests whether the model maintains context and handles dependencies—critical for real conversations.

2. Eight Categories

MT-Bench covers diverse conversation types:

  • Writing: Creative and professional writing tasks (emails, stories, summaries)
  • Roleplay: Acting as a character or expert (travel agent, interviewer, therapist)
  • Reasoning: Logical puzzles, strategic thinking, causal reasoning
  • Math: Problem-solving with calculation and explanation
  • Coding: Writing, debugging, and explaining code
  • Extraction: Pulling structured info from text (names, dates, entities)
  • STEM: Science, technology, engineering questions
  • Humanities: History, philosophy, social sciences

Each category has 10 questions, for 80 total (80 × 2 turns = 160 prompts per model evaluated).

3. GPT-4 as Judge

Each model response is evaluated by GPT-4 on a 1-10 scale. The judge prompt instructs GPT-4 to consider:

  • Helpfulness: Does the response address the user's request?
  • Relevance: Is it on-topic and accurate?
  • Accuracy: Are facts correct (for knowledge/math questions)?
  • Depth: Is the level of detail appropriate?
  • Creativity: Is the response engaging and well-written (for creative tasks)?
  • Level of detail: Not too brief, not too verbose

GPT-4 provides both a numeric score (1-10) and a brief justification for that score.

Example MT-Bench Question

Category: Writing

Turn 1: Compose an engaging travel blog post about a recent trip to Hawaii, highlighting cultural experiences and must-see attractions.

Turn 2: Rewrite your previous response. Start every sentence with the letter 'A'.

Turn 2 tests instruction-following and creativity under constraints. A model that ignores the constraint or produces nonsensical output scores low. A model that successfully rewrites with every sentence starting with 'A' while maintaining coherence scores high.

Scoring and Aggregation

Each turn receives a score from 1-10. Final MT-Bench scores are the average across all 160 turns (80 questions × 2 turns). Models are ranked by this average.

Example leaderboard snapshot (as of paper publication, mid-2023):

  • GPT-4: 8.99 / 10
  • Claude-v1: 7.90 / 10
  • GPT-3.5-turbo: 7.94 / 10
  • Vicuna-13B: 6.39 / 10
  • LLaMA-13B: 4.51 / 10

MT-Bench revealed that instruction-tuned open models (Vicuna) significantly outperformed base models (LLaMA) on conversation quality, even when both had similar MMLU scores.

From the Paper: Key Findings

The MT-Bench paper (Zheng et al., 2023) provides the first rigorous empirical study of LLM judges. Here are the most important results:

Finding 1: GPT-4 Agrees with Humans ~80-85%

The paper reports that "strong LLM judges like GPT-4 can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans." More specifically, on MT-bench with expert judges, "The agreement under setup S2 (w/o tie) between GPT-4 and humans reaches 85%, which is even higher than the agreement among humans (81%)."

On MT-bench pairwise comparisons, GPT-4's judgments matched human preferences around 80-85% of the time (85% in the non-tie setup). Human-human agreement was 81%, meaning GPT-4 is nearly as consistent as humans judging each other.

This result validated LLM-as-a-judge as a practical evaluation method—it's not perfect, but it's good enough to be useful.

Finding 2: Chatbot Arena Correlates with MT-Bench

Chatbot Arena uses real user votes—thousands of crowdsourced pairwise comparisons from the public. These votes are converted into Elo ratings (like chess rankings). The paper demonstrates that Arena Elo ratings and MT-Bench scores show strong agreement, indicating that GPT-4 judge evaluations track real human preferences at scale.

Note: The paper presents correlation analysis between Arena and MT-Bench but does not report a specific Spearman correlation coefficient.

Finding 3: Single-Answer Grading is Less Reliable

The paper tested three judging modes:

  • Pairwise comparison: "Is response A or B better?" (Most reliable)
  • Single-answer grading: "Rate this response on a scale of 1-10." (Less reliable)
  • Reference-guided grading: "Compare this response to a reference answer." (Best for math/factual tasks)

Pairwise comparison had the highest agreement with humans (~80%) because it forces a direct choice. Single-answer grading was more subjective—GPT-4 often gave inflated scores (mean ~7/10), making it harder to differentiate models.

Finding 4: Position Bias is Real but Manageable

The paper identifies position bias as a limitation of LLM judges: "Position bias is when an LLM exhibits a propensity to favor certain positions over others." GPT-4 performs better than other models on consistency, with "Only GPT-4 outputs consistent results in more than 60% of cases" when answers are swapped. The paper proposes swapping positions and averaging as a mitigation strategy.

When judging "Response A vs Response B," GPT-4 slightly prefers whichever is shown first. The solution: evaluate both orders (A vs B and B vs A), then aggregate. This reduces bias from ~60% to ~50% (random baseline).

Finding 5: Verbosity Bias

GPT-4 judges tend to prefer longer responses, even when brevity is more appropriate. This mirrors human annotator biases—longer often feels more "helpful"—but it can be gamed.

Mitigation: Include "appropriate level of detail" in judge prompts, or use length-controlled evaluation (penalize excessive verbosity explicitly).

Finding 6: Self-Enhancement Bias

When GPT-4 judges its own outputs against another model, it shows self-enhancement bias—preferring its own responses more than humans do (~10 percentage point gap).

Implication: Don't use a model to judge itself. Use a separate, stronger judge (e.g., GPT-4 judging GPT-3.5) or multiple judges for cross-validation.

Chatbot Arena: Crowdsourced Preferences at Scale

While MT-Bench uses a fixed set of questions with GPT-4 as judge, Chatbot Arena collects real user preferences on arbitrary prompts from the public.

How Chatbot Arena Works

1. Anonymous Side-by-Side Comparison

Users visit the Arena platform, type a prompt, and receive responses from two anonymous models (labeled "Model A" and "Model B"). After reading both, the user votes for which is better—or declares a tie.

Model identities are hidden until after voting, preventing brand bias (e.g., assuming GPT-4 is better because of its reputation).

2. Elo Rating System

Votes are converted into Elo ratings (borrowed from chess). Each model starts with a baseline rating (e.g., 1500). When Model A beats Model B:

  • Model A gains Elo points (more points if B was rated higher—an "upset")
  • Model B loses Elo points

After thousands of comparisons, Elo ratings converge to reflect true model strength. A 100-point difference means the higher-rated model wins ~64% of matchups; a 200-point difference means ~76%.

3. Bradley-Terry Model

The Elo system is equivalent to the Bradley-Terry preference model from Lesson 04 (DPO):

$$ P(\text{Model A beats Model B}) = \frac{1}{1 + 10^{(R_B - R_A)/400}} $$

Where \(R_A\) and \(R_B\) are Elo ratings. This is the same sigmoid preference model used in RLHF reward training and DPO, connecting evaluation methodology to post-training techniques.

Chatbot Arena Statistics (as of paper publication)

  • Total votes in study: Over 125,000 comparisons (earlier public version had ~70K)
  • Top models: GPT-4, Claude-v1, GPT-3.5 (Elo ratings vary by evaluation period)
  • Prompt diversity: Creative writing, technical questions, roleplay, and general knowledge tasks

Why Arena Matters

Chatbot Arena provides:

  • Unbiased human preferences: No researcher-designed prompts; users bring their real use cases
  • Continuous evaluation: New models can be added to the Arena at any time; rankings update live
  • Validation for LLM judges: Arena Elo correlates with MT-Bench scores, showing that GPT-4 judge is a good proxy for crowd preferences

Arena has become the de facto leaderboard for chat models, complementing static benchmarks like MMLU.

Three LLM Judging Modes

The paper evaluates three ways to use an LLM as a judge. Each has different strengths and failure modes.

Mode 1: Pairwise Comparison

How It Works

Show the judge two responses to the same prompt and ask: "Which is better, A or B (or tie)?"

Example Prompt:

You are a helpful assistant. Compare the following two responses to the user question and determine which is better.

Question: Explain why the sky is blue.

Response A: [Model A's answer]

Response B: [Model B's answer]

Which response is better? Consider helpfulness, accuracy, and clarity. Output only "A", "B", or "Tie".

Strengths

  • Most reliable: 80%+ agreement with humans (highest of all modes)
  • Robust ranking: Repeated pairwise comparisons build consistent leaderboards
  • Matches human evaluation workflow: Humans also find relative comparison easier than absolute scoring

Weaknesses

  • Position bias: Judge may prefer the first response (mitigate by swapping)
  • Quadratic cost: Comparing N models requires O(N²) evaluations
  • No absolute scale: You only know relative ranking, not quality gaps

Mode 2: Single-Answer Grading

How It Works

Show the judge one response and ask for a numeric score (e.g., 1-10).

Example Prompt:

You are a helpful assistant. Rate the following response on a scale of 1 to 10, where 1 is poor and 10 is excellent.

Question: Explain why the sky is blue.

Response: [Model's answer]

Consider helpfulness, accuracy, and clarity. Provide a score and brief justification.

Strengths

  • Efficient: O(N) evaluations for N models
  • Absolute scores: Can measure quality gaps, not just rankings
  • Easier to interpret: "8/10" is more intuitive than "beats Vicuna 65% of the time"

Weaknesses

  • Lower agreement with humans: ~70-75% (humans are inconsistent on absolute scales)
  • Scale calibration issues: GPT-4 tends to give inflated scores (mean ~7/10), compressing the range
  • Verbosity bias: Longer responses get higher scores even when not better

Mode 3: Reference-Guided Grading

How It Works

Provide a reference answer (ground truth or expert response) and ask the judge to compare the model's response to it.

Example Prompt:

You are a helpful assistant. Compare the model's response to the reference answer and rate how well it matches.

Question: What is 15% of 240?

Reference Answer: 36

Model Response: [Model's answer]

Rate from 1-10 how correct and complete the response is. Consider accuracy, explanation quality, and clarity.

Strengths

  • Best for factual/math tasks: Ground truth anchors evaluation, reducing subjectivity
  • Reduces verbosity bias: Judge focuses on correctness, not length
  • Chain-of-thought grading: Can check if reasoning steps are correct, not just the final answer

Weaknesses

  • Requires reference answers: Not applicable to open-ended creative tasks
  • May penalize valid alternative approaches: If the reference uses Method A but the model uses correct Method B, judge might score it lower

Which Mode to Use?

  • Pairwise comparison: Default choice for general chat quality evaluation (most reliable)
  • Single-answer grading: Quick iteration during development when you need fast feedback
  • Reference-guided: Math, coding, extraction, or any task with verifiable ground truth

Judge Biases and How to Mitigate Them

LLM judges aren't perfect. They inherit biases from their training data and prompting. Understanding these biases is critical for building fair evaluation systems.

Bias 1: Position Bias

What It Is

When shown "Response A vs Response B," the judge prefers whichever appears first ~60% of the time, even when both are equal quality.

Why It Happens

LLMs are trained on text where the first option is often correct (e.g., "The answer is A. B is wrong."). This creates an ordering prior that persists in judgment tasks.

Mitigation

  • Swap positions: Evaluate both "A vs B" and "B vs A", then aggregate (if both orders agree, high confidence; if they disagree, mark as tie)
  • Randomize order: Don't always put the baseline first—vary which model is Position 1
  • Few-shot prompting: Include examples in the judge prompt where the second response is better

Bias 2: Verbosity Bias (Length Bias)

What It Is

Judges prefer longer responses, even when brevity is appropriate. On average, responses 50%+ longer than competitors win ~65% of pairwise matchups, regardless of quality.

Why It Happens

Human annotators also prefer longer responses (length correlates with effort and detail). LLM judges inherit this bias. Additionally, verbose responses often include caveats and nuance, which seem more thoughtful.

Mitigation

  • Explicit judge instructions: "Prefer concise answers when appropriate. Penalize unnecessary verbosity."
  • Length-controlled evaluation: Normalize scores by response length or explicitly track length separately
  • Length-controlled benchmarks: Variants like AlpacaEval 2.0 address this by penalizing length in win rate calculations

Bias 3: Self-Enhancement Bias

What It Is

When judging its own outputs vs another model, GPT-4 prefers itself ~10 percentage points more than humans do. Example: if humans prefer GPT-4 over Model X 70% of the time, GPT-4 self-judges prefer itself 80% of the time.

Why It Happens

GPT-4 recognizes its own writing style (phrasing, structure, tone). Even if model outputs are anonymized, subtle patterns leak identity.

Mitigation

  • Never use a model to judge itself: Use a separate judge (e.g., GPT-4 judging Claude or GPT-3.5)
  • Multi-judge consensus: Use multiple LLM judges (GPT-4, Claude, Gemini) and aggregate their votes
  • Blind with paraphrasing: Rewrite responses to mask style before judging (expensive, rarely done in practice)

Bias 4: Sensitivity to Prompt Phrasing

What It Is

Small changes to the judge prompt can swing verdicts by 10-15%. Example: "Which is better?" vs "Which is more helpful?" can produce different rankings.

Mitigation

  • Standardized judge prompts: Use tested, fixed templates (MT-Bench provides theirs)
  • Multi-criteria prompts: Ask judge to consider multiple dimensions (helpfulness, accuracy, clarity) and average
  • Calibration on known pairs: Test your judge prompt on examples with known human labels first

Summary: Mitigating Judge Bias

Best Practices

  • Use pairwise comparison mode (most reliable)
  • Swap positions and aggregate to cancel position bias
  • Include explicit instructions about appropriate detail level
  • Use reference answers for math/code to reduce subjectivity
  • Never let a model judge itself—use a separate, stronger judge
  • Chain-of-thought prompting: Ask the judge to explain its reasoning before rendering verdict (improves consistency)

Agreement Results: Judges vs Humans

How well do LLM judges track human preferences? The MT-Bench paper quantifies this with agreement rate studies.

Methodology

The authors collected 500 pairwise human judgments on MT-Bench questions (crowdworkers voted which of two model responses was better). They then:

  1. Had GPT-4 judge the same 500 pairs
  2. Measured agreement rate: % of pairs where judge and humans agreed
  3. Compared to human-human agreement (two human annotators judging the same pairs)

Results

Judge Agreement with Humans Notes
Human-Human 81% Baseline: How often two humans agree (Table 13)
GPT-4 (pairwise, non-tie) 85% Matches or exceeds human-human agreement (Table 13)
GPT-4 (all votes) ~66-68% Includes ties and position inconsistencies
Claude ~67% Competitive alternative (Table 13)

Interpretation

GPT-4 as Proxy for Human Judgment

Over 80% agreement (85% in non-tie setup) means GPT-4 closely matches human preferences. This approaches or matches human-human agreement (81%).

Implication: GPT-4 disagreeing with a single human annotator is no more concerning than two humans disagreeing with each other. LLM judges are noisy, but so are humans.

Why Not 100%?

The ~20% disagreement comes from:

  • Legitimately subjective preferences: Some pairs have no clear winner (different users want different things)
  • Judge biases: Position bias, verbosity bias contribute to some errors
  • Human annotator noise: Humans make mistakes, rush judgments, or apply inconsistent criteria

Even 70-75% agreement would be useful for coarse model ranking. 80%+ makes LLM judges practical for fine-grained evaluation.

Agreement by Category

Agreement varies by task type:

  • Math: ~90% agreement (most objective—either right or wrong)
  • Coding: ~85% agreement (can verify correctness)
  • Writing/Roleplay: ~75% agreement (highly subjective)
  • Reasoning: ~78% agreement (depends on problem clarity)

Takeaway: LLM judges are most reliable for objective tasks with verifiable correctness. For creative/subjective tasks, agreement is lower but still useful.

Comparison to Automatic Metrics

The paper notes that traditional NLP metrics like BLEU and ROUGE are ineffective for open-ended chat evaluation, as they rely on similarity to reference answers rather than measuring quality directly. LLM judges like GPT-4 achieve much higher agreement with humans (over 80%) compared to these traditional metrics, which typically perform poorly on open-ended generation tasks.

Note: The paper discusses that BLEU and ROUGE are inadequate but does not provide specific agreement percentages for these metrics on MT-bench data. Traditional metrics fundamentally cannot capture helpfulness, instruction-following, or conversational quality the way LLM judges can.

Bridge to Post-Training: Judges, Preferences, and Reward Hacking

LLM-as-judge evaluation isn't just for benchmarking—it's deeply connected to post-training methods from Lessons 02 and 04.

Connection 1: Preference Data Collection

From Evaluation to Training Data

In Lesson 02 (RLHF) and Lesson 04 (DPO), you learned that preference optimization requires preference pairs: (prompt, chosen response, rejected response). Where do these come from?

  • Human labeling (expensive): Hire annotators to rank model outputs
  • LLM judges (scalable): Generate multiple responses per prompt, use GPT-4 to rank them

RLAIF (Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback): The practice of using LLM judges to generate preference data instead of humans. If GPT-4 agrees with humans 80% of the time, then training on GPT-4 preferences should recover 80% of the benefit of training on human preferences—at a fraction of the cost.

Connection 2: The Bradley-Terry Model

Chatbot Arena's Elo system and the Bradley-Terry preference model are the same math used in RLHF reward model training (Lesson 02) and DPO (Lesson 04):

$$ P(\text{Model A preferred over Model B}) = \sigma(r_A - r_B) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(r_A - r_B)}} $$

In Chatbot Arena, \(r_A\) and \(r_B\) are Elo ratings. In RLHF, they're reward model outputs. In DPO, they're implicit rewards \(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}\). All three use the same preference model—evaluation and training are unified by this framework.

Connection 3: GPT-4 as Reward Model

Can You Skip RLHF Reward Training Entirely?

In traditional RLHF (Lesson 02), you train a separate reward model on human preferences, then optimize a policy against that reward model with PPO.

But what if you just use GPT-4 as the reward model directly? During PPO training, query GPT-4 to score each response. This is called reward model-free RLHF or direct RL from LLM feedback.

Pros: No reward model training required; GPT-4 is a high-quality judge.
Cons: Expensive (GPT-4 queries for every PPO rollout), slow, and risk of reward hacking.

Failure Mode: Reward Hacking and Length Exploitation

The Problem

Remember verbosity bias? LLM judges prefer longer responses. If you use an LLM judge as a reward signal for RLHF or DPO, the policy learns to exploit that bias.

Example: A model trained with GPT-4-as-judge learns to generate excessively long, rambling responses padded with filler because that maximizes GPT-4's scores.

This is reward hacking or Goodhart's Law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." The model optimizes the proxy (GPT-4 scores) instead of true quality.

Real-World Example: AlpacaEval and Length-Controlled Variants

AlpacaEval is a popular benchmark that uses GPT-4 as judge to compare models against GPT-3.5. Early versions suffered from severe length exploitation: models could achieve 90%+ win rates just by generating 2-3x longer responses.

AlpacaEval 2.0 (later work) introduced length-controlled win rates: statistically adjust for length differences when computing rankings. This reduced the length gaming significantly.

Lesson: Always monitor response length during training. If length grows without quality improving, you're reward hacking the judge.

Connection 4: Multi-Objective Evaluation

Post-training often balances multiple objectives:

  • Helpfulness: Does the model answer the question?
  • Harmlessness: Does it avoid toxic or unsafe outputs?
  • Honesty: Does it admit uncertainty instead of hallucinating?

MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena conflate these into a single "which is better" judgment. In practice, you might train separate reward models or judges for each dimension, then combine them (e.g., \(r_{\text{total}} = r_{\text{helpful}} + \lambda \cdot r_{\text{harmless}}\)).

Looking Ahead: What Makes a Good Judge?

A good judge for post-training must:

  • Agree with humans highly: 75%+ is baseline; 80%+ is strong
  • Be hard to exploit: No obvious hacks (length, verbosity, buzzwords)
  • Generalize out-of-distribution: Judge novel responses well, not just training-distribution outputs
  • Be consistent: Same response gets same score across multiple evaluations

GPT-4 meets most of these criteria, which is why it's the de facto judge for modern RLHF/DPO systems. But as models improve, judges must too—eventually GPT-4 may not be strong enough to evaluate GPT-5-level models. This is an active research area: scalable oversight and superalignment.
