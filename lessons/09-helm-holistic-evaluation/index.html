Introduction: Beyond Single-Metric Evaluation

In previous lessons, you learned about individual benchmarks: MMLU for knowledge (Lesson 01), GSM8K for reasoning (Lesson 03), HumanEval for code (Lesson 07). Each measures one thing well. But when you deploy a language model in production, you care about everything: accuracy, robustness, fairness, toxicity, efficiency—not just one score.

The HELM Vision

Liang et al. (2022) from Stanford's Center for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM) introduced HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models), a comprehensive benchmark that evaluates 30 prominent language models across 16 core scenarios and 7 metric categories simultaneously.

Key insight: Don't just measure accuracy and call it a day. Measure accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency—for the same scenarios—to expose real tradeoffs and ensure no desideratum gets swept under the rug.

What Makes HELM Different?

HELM isn't just another collection of datasets. It's a systematic evaluation framework built on three pillars:

  • Broad coverage + recognition of incompleteness: Explicitly taxonomize what you evaluate and what you don't, making gaps visible
  • Multi-metric measurement: For each scenario, measure 7 categories of metrics (not just accuracy), achieving 87.5% density across (scenario, metric) pairs
  • Standardization: Evaluate all models on the same scenarios with the same prompting strategy (5-shot), enabling apples-to-apples comparisons

The Problem: Incomplete and Inconsistent Evaluation

1. Single-Metric Myopia

Most benchmarks prioritize accuracy and defer other concerns (fairness, toxicity, robustness) to separate datasets—if they're measured at all. This creates a false sense of progress: a model might score 95% on MMLU but generate toxic content 15% of the time on the same questions.

The RealToxicityPrompts Problem

RealToxicityPrompts (Gehman et al., 2020) is a separate dataset for toxicity. By keeping toxicity evaluation siloed, we miss how toxicity manifests in context—e.g., a model might be accurate on a question about controversial topics but generate biased explanations.

HELM's approach: Measure toxicity, bias, and fairness on the same scenarios where you measure accuracy, making tradeoffs explicit.

2. Narrow Scenario Coverage

Prior to HELM, language model evaluations were fragmented. The paper documents a striking finding: on average, models were evaluated on only 17.9% of HELM's 16 core scenarios, even when aggregating results from multiple papers and sources.

Some prominent models (e.g., T5 11B and Anthropic-LM v4-s3 52B) had zero datasets in common in their original papers. Even when models were evaluated on the same dataset (e.g., HellaSwag), the conditions varied wildly—some used fine-tuning, some used 0-shot prompting, some used 5-shot prompting.

3. Lack of Standardization

Even when two models are evaluated on the same scenario, differences in prompting, number of in-context examples, and adaptation methods make comparisons meaningless. HELM shows that prompt formatting can swing accuracy by 30-50 percentage points on the same model and scenario.

"Prior to HELM, models on average were evaluated on just 17.9% of the core HELM scenarios, with some prominent models not sharing a single scenario in common. We improve this to 96.0%."
— Liang et al., HELM paper §1.1

HELM's Three Pillars of Holistic Evaluation

1. Broad Coverage + Recognition of Incompleteness

HELM starts with a top-down taxonomy of scenarios (task × domain × language) and metrics, then systematically selects from this space. Crucially, it explicitly documents what's missing—e.g., only English is covered; many domain combinations are not instantiated.

Why this matters: You can't fix gaps you don't acknowledge. Transparency about limitations invites the community to fill them.

2. Multi-Metric Measurement

For each of 16 core scenarios, HELM measures 7 categories of metrics: accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency. This yields 98 out of 112 possible (scenario, metric) pairs (87.5% density).

Why this matters: Measuring multiple desiderata together exposes tradeoffs (e.g., accuracy vs robustness) and prevents "accuracy-only" optimization.

3. Standardization

All models are evaluated with 5-shot prompting using simple, generic prompts. Same scenarios, same prompts, same adaptation method. This enables fair head-to-head comparisons.

Why this matters: Prompt engineering can be a competitive advantage, but for benchmark comparison, you want interoperability—models that work well with the same user prompting behavior.

The Taxonomy: Scenarios = Task × Domain × Language

HELM defines a scenario as a triple: (task, domain, language). For example:

  • (question answering, clinical notes from doctors, English)
  • (toxicity detection, tweets from Egypt, Arabic)
  • (summarization, Wikipedia 2018 dump, Spanish)

The paper taxonomizes tasks by surveying ACL 2022 tracks, domains by the 3 W's (what/genre, when/time, who/demographics), and languages by linguistic typology. From this taxonomy, it systematically selects 16 core scenarios prioritizing user-facing tasks and domain diversity.

Core Scenarios Breakdown

Question Answering (9 scenarios): NaturalQuestions (open-book & closed-book), NarrativeQA, QuAC, BoolQ, HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, TruthfulQA, MMLU

Information Retrieval (2): MS MARCO (regular track & TREC track)

Summarization (2): CNN/DailyMail, XSUM

Sentiment Analysis (1): IMDB

Toxicity Detection (1): CivilComments

Miscellaneous Text Classification (1): RAFT (11 ecologically-valid tasks)

Scenarios and Metrics: The 87.5% Density Achievement

7 Metric Categories

HELM measures 7 categories of metrics for each scenario, going far beyond accuracy:

1. Accuracy

Standard performance metrics: exact match for classification, F1 for QA, ROUGE for summarization, MRR/NDCG for retrieval.

2. Calibration

Does the model's probability estimates match reality? Measured via Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and selective classification (abstaining on uncertain examples).

3. Robustness

Worst-case accuracy under perturbations: typos, synonym substitution, contrast sets. Tests if the model relies on spurious correlations.

4. Fairness

Worst-case accuracy across demographic perturbations: dialect variations (AAE vs White English), gender, race. Measured via min/max accuracy across groups.

5. Bias & Stereotypes

Representation of social groups in model generations. Uses metrics like demographic representation and stereotype detection.

6. Toxicity

Rate of toxic content in model generations, measured via PerspectiveAPI. Includes both unprompted toxicity and toxicity in response to toxic prompts.

7. Efficiency

Training cost, inference runtime, memory usage, energy consumption. Includes both "denoised" (real-world API) and "idealized" (controlled hardware) measurements.

Multi-Metric Density: 87.5% of Pairs Measured

Of the 112 possible (core scenario, metric category) pairs, HELM measures 98 pairs (87.5%). The remaining 14 pairs are either undefined (e.g., can't measure toxicity in generations if the scenario doesn't involve generation) or deliberately skipped due to validity concerns.

Targeted Evaluations: 26 Additional Scenarios

Beyond the 16 core scenarios, HELM includes 7 targeted evaluations with 26 additional scenarios to probe specific capabilities and risks:

  • Linguistic understanding: Language modeling on The Pile, TwitterAAE, ICE; minimal pairs (BLiMP)
  • Knowledge: Fact completion (WikiFact), knowledge-intensive QA (TruthfulQA, MMLU)
  • Reasoning: Synthetic reasoning primitives, GSM8K, MATH, HumanEval, legal/logical reasoning
  • Memorization/Copyright: Verbatim extraction from BooksCorpus, bestsellers, Linux kernel
  • Disinformation: Narrative reiteration, narrative wedging
  • Bias: BBQ dataset (9 demographic categories)
  • Toxicity: RealToxicityPrompts, BOLD

Of these 26 targeted scenarios, 21 were new or not previously used in mainstream LM evaluation, expanding the evaluation frontier significantly.

Key Results: What HELM Revealed

1. The Instruction-Tuning Advantage

text-davinci-002 and Anthropic-LM v4-s3 (52B) were the only instruction-tuned models evaluated. They dominated on accuracy, robustness, and fairness—despite Anthropic-LM being 10× smaller than the largest models (TNLG v2 530B).

"text-davinci-002 performs best on our accuracy, robustness, and fairness metrics, with Anthropic-LM v4-s3 (52B) being in the top 3 for all 3 metrics (despite being more than 10× smaller in model scale compared to TNLG v2 (530B))."
— Liang et al., §1.2, Finding 1

2. Open vs Non-Open Gap

At the time of the paper (November 2022), there was a consistent gap between open models and non-open models (closed or limited-access) across all core scenarios. The top performers were text-davinci-002 (limited-access), Anthropic-LM v4-s3 (closed), and TNLG v2 530B (closed).

However, the paper notes that recent releases of open models (OPT 175B, BLOOM 176B, GLM 130B) had greatly reduced this gap over the preceding year.

3. Accuracy-Robustness-Fairness Correlations (and Exceptions)

Across scenarios, accuracy, robustness, and fairness are strongly correlated—models that do well on accuracy tend to also be robust and fair. However, there are important exceptions:

  • On NarrativeQA, TNLG v2 (530B) had 72.6% standard accuracy but precipitously dropped to 38.9% accuracy under robustness perturbations.
  • On TwitterAAE, OPT (175B) degraded from 1.506 bits/byte for White English to 2.114 bits/byte for African American English (40% worse perplexity).

4. Prompt Sensitivity is Extreme

All models showed significant sensitivity to prompt formatting, choice of in-context examples, and number of examples. The paper documents:

Prompt Sensitivity Examples

  • OPT (175B) on HellaSwag: 79.1% accuracy with separate prompts (one per answer choice) vs 30.2% accuracy with joint prompts (multiple-choice format)—a 49-point swing!
  • Multiple-choice adaptation: Different models perform best with different prompt formats for the same scenario, making "fair" standardization challenging.
  • Random seed for in-context examples: Results vary significantly across different random selections of the same 5 examples.

5. Calibration Depends on Scenario

The relationship between accuracy and calibration varies by scenario and adaptation method:

  • HellaSwag: Improving accuracy worsens calibration
  • OpenBookQA: Improving accuracy improves calibration

This suggests calibration cannot be treated as a simple by-product of accuracy—it must be measured scenario-by-scenario.

6. Summarization Metrics Fail to Discriminate

On CNN/DailyMail and XSUM, automated summarization metrics (ROUGE, BERTScore) largely failed to discriminate differences in model quality that the authors observed qualitatively. This highlights the ongoing challenge of evaluation for open-ended generation tasks.

7. Code Models Dominate Reasoning

code-davinci-002 consistently outperformed text models on reasoning tasks, even on synthetic reasoning in natural language:

  • GSM8K: code-davinci-002 achieved 52.1% accuracy vs 35.0% for text-davinci-002 (next best) and <16% for all others
  • Synthetic reasoning: code-davinci-002 scored 72.7% vs text-davinci-002's 65.1% and OPT (175B)'s 29.4%

This suggests that training on code may improve structured reasoning abilities more broadly.

Evaluation Scale

HELM evaluated 30 models × 42 scenarios × multiple metrics = 4,939 runs. This consumed 12.2 billion tokens, 17.4 million queries, $38,001 for commercial APIs, and ~19,500 GPU hours for open models.

Models ranged from 350M parameters (OpenAI ada) to 530B parameters (TNLG v2), spanning 12 organizations: AI21 Labs, Anthropic, BigScience, Cohere, EleutherAI, Google, Meta, Microsoft/NVIDIA, OpenAI, Tsinghua University, and Yandex.

Bridge to Post-Training & The Curriculum

How HELM Connects to Post-Training (Lessons 02, 04, 06, 08)

In Track B, you learned about SFT, RLHF, DPO, GRPO, and Constitutional AI—techniques to improve models after pretraining. HELM is how you know if they worked.

RLHF (Lesson 02)

InstructGPT showed 1.3B > 175B GPT-3 on human evals. But human evals are expensive and don't cover robustness, fairness, or toxicity. HELM would measure all 7 desiderata across 16 scenarios to confirm RLHF improved the model holistically, not just on helpfulness.

DPO (Lesson 04)

DPO claims to match RLHF without PPO. But at what cost to fairness? Robustness? HELM's multi-metric lens exposes tradeoffs—maybe DPO improves accuracy but hurts calibration or fairness. You need to measure them together.

GRPO (Lesson 06)

DeepSeekMath used GRPO with verifiable math rewards. HELM's reasoning evaluations (GSM8K, MATH) would measure accuracy, and robustness/fairness metrics would tell you if the RL training introduced brittleness or demographic disparities.

Constitutional AI (Lesson 08)

CAI reduced harmfulness via critique-revision and RLAIF. But did it reduce toxicity at the cost of helpfulness or accuracy? HELM's multi-metric evaluation on realistic scenarios (QA, summarization, sentiment) would expose the Pareto frontier.

Why Multi-Metric Matters for Post-Training

Post-training techniques (SFT, RLHF, DPO, GRPO, CAI) optimize for specific objectives—usually reward model scores, human preferences, or verifiable correctness. But what you optimize for is not always what you care about in deployment:

  • Goodhart's Law: Over-optimizing the reward model can degrade actual quality (reward hacking)
  • Alignment tax: Making models safer can hurt capability (accuracy, reasoning)
  • Robustness collapse: RL can make models brittle to small input changes
  • Fairness regressions: Improving average accuracy can widen demographic gaps

HELM's 87.5% density across 7 metrics catches these tradeoffs. You can't hide robustness degradation or fairness regressions if you're required to report them alongside accuracy.

From Single Benchmarks to Holistic Evaluation

The curriculum journey so far:

  • Lesson 01 (MMLU): Knowledge benchmarks—coverage, contamination, multiple-choice artifacts
  • Lesson 03 (GSM8K): Reasoning benchmarks—multi-step, verifiable, hard to saturate
  • Lesson 05 (MT-Bench): Open-ended eval—LLM-as-judge, pairwise, biases
  • Lesson 07 (HumanEval): Code eval—functional correctness, pass@k, temperature tuning
  • Lesson 09 (HELM): Integrate everything. Don't just run one benchmark—run 16 core scenarios with 7 metrics each, exposing tradeoffs and ensuring no desideratum gets swept under the rug.
