Why Holistic Evaluation Matters

As language models become the foundation for almost all major language technologies, their capabilities, limitations, and risks must be well understood. But a critical problem emerged: models were being evaluated inconsistently across different scenarios, making comparisons difficult and obscuring important trade-offs.

Before HELM, the evaluation landscape was fragmented:

  • Different models were evaluated on different benchmarks
  • Evaluation focused overwhelmingly on accuracy, sidelining other important properties
  • Models averaged just 17.9% coverage across what would become HELM's core scenarios
  • Some prominent models didn't share a single evaluation scenario in common

HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models) addresses this by providing a standardized, comprehensive framework that evaluates models across many scenarios and metrics under consistent conditions.

What is HELM?

HELM is both a benchmark and an evaluation framework developed by Stanford's Center for Research on Foundation Models (CRFM). It evaluates language models across:

HELM at a Glance

  • 30 models evaluated from 12 organizations (OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google, etc.)
  • 42 scenarios total: 16 core scenarios + 26 targeted evaluation scenarios
  • 7 metric categories: accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, efficiency
  • 21 new scenarios introduced that hadn't been used in mainstream LM evaluation
  • 4,939 evaluation runs totaling ~12 billion tokens and 17 million queries
  • Coverage improvement: from 17.9% to 96.0% of core scenarios per model

The Three Pillars of HELM

1. Broad Coverage via Taxonomy

HELM starts with a systematic taxonomy of scenarios (use cases) and metrics (desiderata). Rather than ad-hoc dataset collection, HELM explicitly documents what it evaluates and what's missing, making gaps transparent.

2. Multi-Metric Measurement

For each of the 16 core scenarios, HELM measures all 7 metric categories where applicable (achieving 98 of 112 possible scenario×metric pairs, or 87.5% coverage). This ensures metrics beyond accuracy—like fairness, robustness, and toxicity—are not treated as second-class citizens.

3. Standardized Evaluation

All 30 models are evaluated under the same conditions using standardized prompting strategies (primarily 5-shot prompting). This enables direct head-to-head comparisons that were previously impossible when models were evaluated inconsistently.

From the Paper: Core Insights

The HELM paper (Liang et al., 2022) provides unprecedented transparency into language model capabilities. Here are key insights directly from the paper:

Abstract (Key Excerpt)

"We measure 7 metrics (accuracy, calibration, robustness, fairness, bias, toxicity, and efficiency) for each of 16 core scenarios to the extent possible (87.5% of the time), ensuring that metrics beyond accuracy don't fall to the wayside, and that trade-offs across models and metrics are clearly exposed."

The Coverage Problem

The paper documents exactly how inconsistent model evaluation was before HELM:

"Prior to HELM, models on average were evaluated on just 17.9% of the core HELM scenarios, with some prominent models not sharing a single scenario in common. We improve this to 96.0%: now all 30 models have been densely benchmarked on a set of core scenarios and metrics under standardized conditions."

Scale of the Evaluation

From the paper's empirical findings section:

"To give a sense of the magnitude of our evaluation, we ran a total of 4,939 runs (i.e. evaluating a specific model on a specific scenario). This amounts to a total cost of 12,169,227,491 tokens and 17,431,479 queries across all models."

The Seven Metrics

HELM measures seven categories of metrics for each core scenario. This multi-metric approach makes explicit the trade-offs between different desiderata:

1. Accuracy

Task-specific performance: exact match, F1, ROUGE, MRR, NDCG depending on the scenario.

2. Calibration

How well model confidence matches actual correctness. Critical for high-stakes decisions where systems need to know when to defer to humans.

3. Robustness

Performance consistency under perturbations: typos (invariance), paraphrases (equivariance), dialect variations.

4. Fairness

Performance parity across demographic groups. Tests whether models perform equally well for questions mentioning different genders or races.

5. Bias

Stereotypical associations in model outputs. Measures representation disparities and harmful stereotypes about demographics and professions.

6. Toxicity

Rate of generating harmful, offensive, or inappropriate content, measured using Perspective API.

7. Efficiency

Computational cost: inference time, training FLOPs. Important for deployment and environmental impact.

Coverage Matrix: 98 of 112 Pairs Measured

HELM's core evaluation measures 98 out of 112 possible (scenario × metric) pairs, achieving 87.5% coverage. The 14 unmeasured pairs are either not well-defined (e.g., can't measure toxicity of completions when the task doesn't involve generation) or excluded due to validity concerns (e.g., robustness perturbations for long-form summarization outputs).

Key Findings from the Paper

HELM's comprehensive evaluation surfaced 25 top-level findings. Here are some of the most striking discoveries:

Finding #1: No Single Model Dominates

"No single model dominates across all scenarios and metrics."

Different models excel at different tasks: text-davinci-002 leads on knowledge tasks, code-davinci-002 on reasoning, but no model is best everywhere. This underscores the importance of evaluating across diverse scenarios.

Finding #16: Code Models Excel at Reasoning

The paper's reasoning analysis reveals a striking pattern:

"For reasoning-intensive scenarios, we find that the code models, especially code-davinci-002, consistently outperform the text models, even on synthetic reasoning scenarios posed in natural language. This gap is made clear in mathematical reasoning: for GSM8K, code-davinci-002 achieves an accuracy of 52.1%, where the next best model is text-davinci-002 at 35.0% and no other model surpasses 16%."

Finding #23: Extreme Prompt Sensitivity

One of HELM's most concerning findings is the extreme sensitivity of model performance to prompt formatting:

"We find that model performance is extremely sensitive to how multiple choice scenarios are adapted into prompts: for example, accuracy for OPT (175B) on HellaSwag is 79.1% when each answer choice is presented in a separate 0-shot prompt (i.e. one of the most accurate models), but drops precipitously to 30.2% (almost random accuracy) when the answer choices are presented jointly in a single 5-shot prompt (i.e. in the format of a multiple-choice exam)."

This drop from 79.1% to 30.2% represents a catastrophic failure—the same model on the same task achieves top-tier performance or near-random accuracy purely based on prompt structure. This finding challenges what it means to standardize evaluation fairly across models.

Finding #7: Accuracy vs. Efficiency

"We do not see a strong trade-off between accuracy and efficiency (which depends on both the model architecture and the hardware) across all 30 models. For each family of models (e.g. different size variants of GPT-3), we find that as models become larger, accuracy consistently improves but with higher training and inference cost."

Within model families, there's a clear accuracy-cost curve. But across families, only a subset of models sit on the Pareto frontier, suggesting room for architectures that achieve better accuracy-efficiency trade-offs.

Limitations and Future Directions

HELM is comprehensive, but the authors acknowledge several limitations:

Language Coverage

HELM currently covers only English (though it includes several English varieties like African-American English and English from different countries). Expanding to other languages is a priority for future work.

Scenario Selection

While HELM's taxonomy makes explicit what's covered and what's missing, the 42 scenarios still represent a subset of possible use cases. The paper notes gaps in question answering for neglected dialects and metrics for certain aspects of trustworthiness.

Evolving Models

HELM evaluated live systems that may update over time. Some models (especially commercial APIs) may have changed during the evaluation period or since publication. The paper records timestamps but acknowledges this introduces some uncertainty.

Prompt Sensitivity

The extreme sensitivity to prompting (Finding #23) poses a fundamental challenge: what does "fair" standardized evaluation mean when models achieve radically different results based on prompt format? HELM standardizes prompting, but optimal prompting may differ across models.

Living Benchmark

HELM is designed as a living benchmark, continuously updated with new scenarios, metrics, and models at crfm.stanford.edu/helm. The community can propose new scenarios and contribute to the framework.

Connecting to Post-Training

HELM's holistic approach has critical implications for post-training work (RLHF, DPO, etc.) that we've studied in Track B:

Multi-Metric Trade-offs in RLHF

HELM makes explicit that optimizing for one metric (e.g., accuracy or reward model score) may harm others. This connects directly to:

  • Alignment tax (Lesson 02): Does RLHF improve helpfulness while degrading accuracy on knowledge tasks?
  • Reward hacking: Are models learning to game the reward without improving true quality?
  • Safety vs. capability: Constitutional AI (Lesson 08) seeks non-evasive harmlessness, measured through both toxicity AND helpfulness

Code Models and Verifiable Rewards

HELM's Finding #16 (code-davinci-002 excels at reasoning) connects to DeepSeekMath and GRPO (Lesson 06). Code pretraining may help models learn structured reasoning, and verifiable rewards (like math correctness) avoid the pitfalls of learned reward models.

Evaluation for Preference Learning

DPO and RLHF (Lessons 02, 04) require evaluation to know if preference learning succeeds. HELM's standardized scenarios provide consistent benchmarks to measure:

  • Does preference learning improve win rates on MT-Bench style evaluations?
  • Does it maintain performance on accuracy benchmarks?
  • Does it introduce or reduce biases?

Prompt Engineering and Adaptation

Finding #23's extreme prompt sensitivity has implications for instruction tuning and SFT. If models are so brittle to prompt format, post-training methods must either:

  • Improve robustness to diverse prompts
  • Provide clear guidelines on optimal prompting
  • Or risk deploying models that work wonderfully in one format and fail catastrophically in another

Key Concepts

  • Scenario: A (task, domain, language) triple representing a use case
  • Metric/Desiderata: A property we want to measure (accuracy, fairness, etc.)
  • Adaptation: How we convert a scenario into prompts for the model
  • Run: Evaluating one model on one scenario with one adaptation method
  • Multi-metric: Measuring many desiderata for the same scenario
  • Holistic: Comprehensive coverage of scenarios and metrics with explicit taxonomy

Discussion Questions

  • If prompt formatting causes 79% → 30% accuracy swings, how can we trust any single evaluation number?
  • Should "standardization" mean the same prompt for all models, or optimal prompts per model?
  • HELM measures 7 metrics, but are there important desiderata still missing?
  • How should we weigh accuracy vs. fairness vs. efficiency when choosing a model to deploy?
  • Can a living benchmark stay meaningful if it's continuously updated and models are evaluated at different times?
